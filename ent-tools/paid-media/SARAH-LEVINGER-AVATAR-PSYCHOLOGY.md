## THE CORE PROBLEM WITH MOST AVATARS

> "Almost every avatar I've ever seen is demographic information — a 25 to 45 year old male who lives in Chicago with two cats. It's very clinical. Very sterile."

**The real problem: marketer bias.**

Most brands build their avatar around whoever the marketer is most biased toward — usually the loudest customer, not the highest-revenue customer. This creates a feedback loop:
- You market to who you think your customer is
- Those people respond
- You think your hypothesis is confirmed
- But you've just been fishing in the same pond

**The chicken and egg trap:**
> "Is that your actual customer — or is that your customer because that's who you're marketing to the most because you're biased toward them?"

**The Lomi example (Matt's brand):**
- Loudest customer: Sandy (older, environmental, shows up in Facebook groups, writes the emails, leaves the reviews)
- Actual biggest revenue customer: 40-something moms with Young kids, food waste guilt, NOT environmentalists
- They built all their marketing around Sandy and ignored the bigger segment

**The Paila case (Sean's brand):**
- Launched targeting zero-wasters (super loud, had YouTube channels, were "psychos" in the best way)
- Real customer that arrived: "Slacktivist" — "I know a little bit about climate and I don't like it, so I'll support this brand"
- The loud customer was the foot in the door, not the real buyer

## THE 5 DIMENSIONS OF AVATAR RESEARCH

Sarah's framework for going beyond demographics:

### 1. IDENTITY
What does this person believe about themselves? What labels do they wear?

Not: "she's a mom who likes fitness"
But: "she identifies as someone who takes care of herself — and that identity is under threat when her skin/body/health doesn't reflect who she believes she is"

Identity questions to answer:
- What words would they use to describe themselves?
- What communities do they belong to (even aspirationally)?
- What do they want others to see them as?
- What makes them feel pride? What makes them feel like a fraud?

### 2. EMOTION
What specific emotion is driving the purchase? (These are NOT interchangeable)

| Emotion | What it looks like | Different from |
|---------|-------------------|----------------|
| Shame | "I am the problem" — self-directed | Guilt |
| Guilt | "I did something wrong" — act-directed | Shame |
| Disgust | "This is gross / unacceptable" | Both |
| Fear | "Something bad will happen" | The above |
| Pride | "I want others to see this" | The above |
| Aspiration | "I want to become X" | The above |

**The Lomi "food waste is gross" example:**
- The headline worked — best performing ever — but Matt didn't know WHY
- Sarah's diagnosis: The word "gross" may have attached not to the food, but to the PERSON
- The customer felt disgusted with themselves for wasting food, not just the food itself
- Disgust (self-directed) is a different — and more powerful — lever than guilt

> "Do they feel ashamed of how much food they're throwing away, or do they feel disgusted with themselves as a person that they can't solve this problem? Two completely different emotions."

### 3. GENERATION
How someone was raised directly shapes their buying behavior and emotional triggers.

**The Millennial/Gen X insight from the podcast:**
- Gen X/Old Millennials: raised by two-income boomer parents who were often absent
- Result: emotional neglect → carried shame and guilt into adulthood
- These adults respond to: "it's not your fault" messaging, validation that their behavior is understandable
- The most resonant Lomi messaging isn't about the planet — it's about the shame of throwing out food that "your parents worked hard to afford"

Questions to answer per generation:
- What did they grow up being told about themselves?
- What cultural events shaped their worldview (e.g., 2008 recession, 9/11, pandemic)?
- What did their parents value? What did they rebel against?
- What childhood emotional patterns do they carry into adult purchasing?

### 4. SEASONAL PURCHASING
When do they buy? What contextual events trigger purchase?

- New Year (identity reset)
- Post-summer (recovery)
- Back to school (routine rebuilding)
- Pre-event (wedding, reunion, vacation)
- Life stage trigger (new job, breakup, postpartum)

### 5. CULTURAL MOVEMENTS
What is culture doing to your category? Are you riding a wave or fighting one?

**The De Beers story:**
- Sapphires were #1 for engagement rings before De Beers
- De Beers didn't just advertise — they first seeded IDENTITY (get diamonds on celebrities, let it trickle down) THEN hit EMOTION ("A Diamond is Forever")
- Two-phase brand building: Identity first → Emotion second
- You can't sell to an emotion that doesn't have an identity attached to it yet

**The Ferrari vs. Lamborghini story:**
- Ferrari: exclusivity strategy → stopped selling to new money buyers
- Lamborghini: accessible → bought by dropshippers who needed to signal wealth → became the cultural icon
- Lesson: reach builds identity, identity builds culture, culture builds brand. Ferrari's exclusivity killed their cultural moment.

## HOW TO BUILD AVATARS CORRECTLY

### Step 1: Start with your loudest customer — then question them

Your loudest customer is the person who:
- Leaves the reviews
- Shows up in Facebook groups
- Sends the fan emails
- Tells all their friends

They are NOT necessarily your biggest revenue customer. Use them as a starting hypothesis only.

### Step 2: Name them — but name them with meaning

Good names create shorthand for your whole team. Two approaches:
- **Acronym (Sean's method):** "Ed" = Everyday Dad. Forces you to define what the name STANDS for. Can't propose a new customer type without an acronym.
- **Day-in-the-life (Matt's method):** "Olive" — maps her entire day from wake to sleep. Experiential, subjective.

Both are valid. Different angles produce different creative insights:
- Objective/activity-based names → better for ad targeting and channel selection
- Experiential/day-in-the-life names → better for ad copy, hooks, and emotional resonance

### Step 3: Run surveys to remove bias

**The bias problem:** If you only survey your email list, you're surveying people who already like you. You get a warm audience perspective that will diverge significantly from cold traffic on identity and aspirational questions.

**Sarah's method:**
- Run the same survey to: (1) email list, (2) cold paid ad traffic with no brand mention
- Surface questions (what do you buy, how old are you) — answers will be similar
- Identity/aspiration questions (what do you want most, how do you see yourself) — will diverge dramatically
- The divergence tells you the difference between who you think you're selling to and who would actually buy

### Step 4: Study 5 dimensions per avatar

For each avatar:
- [ ] Identity: what do they believe about themselves?
- [ ] Emotion: what specific emotion drives the purchase?
- [ ] Generation: how does their upbringing shape their behavior?
- [ ] Seasonal: when do they buy and why?
- [ ] Cultural: what cultural moment does this product fit?

### Step 5: Map the cohorts inside your core avatar

You have a flagship core customer — but inside that avatar there are multiple sub-types.

**Sean's "Ted" (Tech-Enabled Ted) example:**
Inside "Ted" you have:
- PC Master Race guys
- Mac guys
- High-end vinyl collectors
These are all Teds but they require different messaging.

**The framework:** Flagship avatar → Sub-cohorts → Individual messaging per cohort

## THE LOUD CUSTOMER TRAP (Critical for DTC Brands)

> "The interesting part is she just happens to be the loudest customer that we have — whereas like the bulk of the Lomi customer is actually somebody in their 40s who has young kids."

**Signs you're trapped:**
- Your ad imagery skews toward your "loudest" customer's aesthetic
- Your review responses are all from a specific persona type
- Your team talks about "your customer" as if there's only one
- Your messaging hasn't evolved in 18 months

**How to escape:**
- Pull your top 20% revenue customers by lifetime value
- Build a profile from their actual behavior (what they bought, when, what they said in reviews)
- Ask: does this match who we THINK our customer is?

## THE OBJECTIVE vs. SUBJECTIVE AVATAR SPLIT

Two valid approaches that produce different marketing outputs:

**Objective (Activity/Identity-based — Sean's approach)**
- What they do
- Their hobbies and interests
- What products/brands they already buy
- What media they consume
- Yields: targeting insights, channel selection, partnership decisions

**Subjective (Experiential/Day-in-the-life — Matt's approach)**
- Her morning routine
- How she feels walking into work
- What stresses her out at 2pm
- What she does when she wants to decompress
- Yields: copy insights, hook ideas, emotional resonance, UGC briefs

**Best practice: do both.**
The objective tells you WHERE to find her. The subjective tells you WHAT to say.

## THE DE BEERS FRAMEWORK (Identity → Emotion Sequencing)

**Phase 1: Build Identity**
Get your product into the hands/minds of people who represent the identity you want your customer to aspire to. This creates cultural familiarity and permission.

**Phase 2: Hit Emotion**
Once identity is established, anchor the product to a specific, singular emotion. "A Diamond is Forever" = the emotion of commitment, permanence, love.

**For DTC paid media:**
- Cold traffic ads should build IDENTITY (make her see this product as for someone like her)
- Warm traffic / retargeting ads should hit EMOTION (the specific feeling she gets from having/using it)
- You cannot skip Phase 1 and jump straight to Phase 2 with cold audiences

**The key addition to persona cards:**
Each persona needs not just "who she is" and "what made her act" — she needs:
- **Identity**: what she believes about herself
- **Specific emotion**: the single emotion driving the purchase (not "she feels bad" — shame? guilt? disgust? fear?)
- **Generational driver**: what in her upbringing amplifies that emotion
- **Loudness rating**: is this persona loud (shows up in comments) or quiet (buys without talking)?
- **De Beers phase**: does she need identity-building first (cold) or is she emotion-ready (warm)?
